# Thesis-ready methods and results

## Methods

Genetic ancestry assignments and the first five genetic principal components were obtained from the retained SG100K GRID sample annotation. These assignments comprised core Chinese, Indian and Malay categories, three pairwise intermediate categories (Chinese–Malay, Chinese–Indian and Indian–Malay), and an Other category. Recorded ethnicity was evaluated using two distinct variables: race recorded from the Singapore National Registration Identity Card (NRIC; `FREG5_Race`) and participant-described ethnic heritage from the interviewer-administered questionnaire (`FIAQ10_Demo1`). Parental ethnic heritage was used as a secondary validation of the pairwise intermediate assignments. Concordance was summarised as an exact core match, a pairwise intermediate assignment involving the recorded group, or a genetic ancestry label not involving the recorded group. Group-specific proportions were reported with 95% Wilson confidence intervals and compared using Pearson chi-square and two-sided Fisher exact tests. The retained 10k and 22k analysis files were linked back to the GRID annotation to establish how genetic ancestry categories mapped to the ancestry-specific GWAS inputs. No participant-level outputs were exported.

The retained 22k cognitive phenotype was additionally reconstructed and tested under four alternative specifications: adjustment for all seven GRID categories, restriction to the three core GRID categories used in the ancestry-specific GWASs, additional adjustment for participant-described ethnic heritage within the questionnaire subset, and restriction to participants whose core GRID assignment matched their self-description. Each alternative model retained the original demographic and education covariates. Sensitivity was evaluated using Tucker congruence of PC1 loadings, participant-score correlations on overlapping samples and the change in task-level explained variance.

## Results

Among 23,615 genetically annotated HELIOS participants, the GRID assignment identified 18,905 core Chinese, 2,481 core Indian, 1,367 core Malay, 386 Chinese–Malay intermediate, 179 Chinese–Indian intermediate, 181 Indian–Malay intermediate and 116 Other participants. Participant-described ethnic heritage was available for 13,906 participants (58.9%), with differential availability among NRIC-recorded Chinese (65.4%), Indian (34.4%) and Malay (26.0%) participants.

Within the questionnaire subset, exact correspondence with the relevant core GRID group was 98.87% among self-described Chinese participants, 92.99% among self-described Indian participants and 75.84% among self-described Malay participants. Pairwise intermediate assignments involving the participant-described group occurred in 1.00%, 5.37% and 17.46%, respectively. A GRID label that did not involve the participant-described group occurred in 0.13% of Chinese, 1.64% of Indian and 6.70% of Malay participants. These proportions differed across groups (Pearson chi-square = 447.61, df = 2, p = 6.35 × 10^-98). Thus, the larger number of visually unusual Chinese points did not represent a higher rate of discordance; self-described Chinese participants had the lowest rate after accounting for group size. Because questionnaire availability differed by NRIC-recorded race, these estimates describe the observed questionnaire subset rather than the complete HELIOS sample.

Among participants with both parental ethnic-heritage variables recorded, one Chinese and one Indian parent were reported by 27 of 65 (41.5%) participants in the Chinese–Indian intermediate GRID category. The corresponding parental-pair matches were 21 of 152 (13.8%) for the Chinese–Malay category and 14 of 61 (23.0%) for the Indian–Malay category. These findings provide the strongest direct support for recent mixed parental ancestry in the Chinese–Indian intermediate category. The other pairwise GRID categories should be described more conservatively as intermediate genetic ancestry because they may also reflect older admixture, continuous population structure or the limitations of broad parental categories.

The retained 10k and combined 22k analysis ancestry variables mapped exclusively to the corresponding core GRID categories. No participant assigned to a pairwise intermediate or Other GRID category received a Chinese, Indian or Malay label in the combined 22k analysis table. The GWAS ancestry subsets should therefore be described as genetic-analysis groups derived from the GRID assignments, rather than as self-reported ethnic groups.

The pooled 22k phenotype PCA nevertheless included 826 participants (3.68%) assigned to a pairwise intermediate or Other GRID category. Because the retained phenotype model used only Indian and Malay indicators, these participants entered its Chinese reference coding. Correcting this by including all seven GRID categories left the PC1 loading pattern unchanged (Tucker congruence = 0.99999994) and produced scores correlated r = 0.999851 with the retained scores. Restricting phenotype construction to the three core GRID groups similarly preserved the loadings (congruence = 0.99998224) and scores (r = 0.999882).

Participant-described ethnic heritage was available for 13,102 members of the core-GRID phenotype sample. Adding this variable to the otherwise identical phenotype model produced loading congruence of 0.99999997 and a score correlation of r = 0.999729. Although participant-described ethnic heritage accounted for small additional proportions of variance in working memory and pairing guesses after FDR correction, the increments were less than 0.11 percentage points. Excluding the 113 records whose core GRID assignment differed from their self-description also left the factor unchanged (loading congruence = 0.99999884; score r = 0.999982).

## Interpretation

Self-described ethnic heritage, NRIC-recorded race and genome-wide genetic ancestry are related but non-equivalent constructs. The three major genetic clusters broadly corresponded to recorded Chinese, Indian and Malay categories, while the GRID annotation also captured pairwise intermediate genetic structure. The results do not indicate that self-described Chinese participants were disproportionately discordant from the supplied genetic ancestry assignment. No inference can be made from these data about why participants used a particular ethnic label.

The phenotype sensitivities indicate that neither the original reference-category coding nor omission of participant-described ethnic heritage materially affected the cognitive factor. The retained phenotype can therefore be used for the primary genetic analyses, provided that the thesis distinguishes genetic ancestry from administratively recorded race and participant-described ethnic heritage and avoids treating genetic ancestry groups as homogeneous sociocultural categories.
